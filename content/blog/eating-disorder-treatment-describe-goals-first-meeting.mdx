# Eating Disorder Treatment: How to Describe Your Goals at the First Meeting

Starting eating disorder treatment can feel both hopeful and overwhelming. The first meeting with a provider is a chance to share what you are experiencing and what you hope to change. Yet many people leave that conversation wishing they had said more—or said it differently. This guide is designed to help you prepare to describe your goals clearly, so the provider can better understand your priorities and collaborate with you on a plan.

This article offers general information, not medical advice, diagnosis, or treatment recommendations. Eating disorders are complex, and only qualified professionals can assess your situation and suggest appropriate care. If you are in crisis, please contact a local emergency service or a trusted healthcare professional immediately.

## Why Describing Goals Matters

Treatment works best when it is tailored to the person. Providers rely on what you share to understand your concerns, values, and what you want to achieve. When you can articulate your goals—even if they feel messy or uncertain—you give the provider a clearer starting point. This can lead to a more collaborative relationship and a plan that feels more relevant to your life.

Goals are not just about symptoms. They can include how you want to feel, how you want to spend your time, how you want to relate to others, and what kind of support you need. You do not need to have everything figured out. It is okay to say, "I am not sure yet, but I know I want things to be different."

## A Checklist to Prepare for the First Meeting

Before your appointment, consider these steps. You can write notes or simply think them through. There is no right or wrong way.

- **Identify what prompted you to seek help now.** What changed, or what has been building up? This helps the provider understand urgency and context.
- **List the areas of your life that feel most affected.** For example: eating, body image, mood, relationships, work or school, physical health, sleep, or daily routines.
- **Think about what you want more of, not just less of.** Instead of only "stop bingeing," you might want "more energy to see friends" or "feeling less preoccupied with food."
- **Consider your values.** What matters to you? Honesty, independence, creativity, connection, health, stability? Goals that align with your values can feel more motivating.
- **Note any fears or hesitations.** It is normal to feel ambivalent. Sharing that can help the provider address concerns and adjust the pace.
- **Write down questions you have.** About the provider's approach, what sessions look like, how progress is measured, or anything else.
- **Decide what you are comfortable sharing initially.** You do not have to disclose everything at once. You can say, "I am not ready to talk about that yet."
- **Bring a support person if that feels helpful.** Sometimes a trusted friend or family member can help you remember details or offer another perspective.
- **Plan for practical details.** How will you get to appointments? Do you need remote options? What times work? These are part of your goals too.
- **Be gentle with yourself.** The first meeting is a conversation, not a test.

## How to Put Goals into Words

You can describe goals in many ways. Here are some sentence starters that might help:

- "I want to be able to..."
- "I hope to feel less..."
- "It is important to me that..."
- "I would like to work on..."
- "My main concern right now is..."
- "I am not sure where to start, but I know I want..."

You can also use a scale. For example: "On a scale of 1 to 10, how important is it to you to reduce binge episodes?" Or "How confident do you feel that you can make changes in your eating right now?" These can help both you and the provider understand your priorities.

Remember that goals can be short-term or long-term. You might have a goal for the next month, like "eat breakfast three times a week," and a longer-term goal like "rebuild trust with my partner." Both are valid.

## Concrete Examples (Clearly Labeled as Hypothetical)

The following examples are hypothetical and for illustration only. They are not based on any real person and are not intended as treatment advice.

**Example 1: Focusing on daily functioning**

"I have been skipping lunch most days because I am worried about calories. It is making me tired and distracted at work. My goal is to feel steady enough to get through the afternoon without crashing. I also want to stop obsessing about what I ate earlier in the day. I am not ready to talk about weight yet, but I do want to talk about how to make meals feel less stressful."

**Example 2: Addressing binge episodes and shame**

"I binge at night, and then I feel awful about myself. I want to understand why this happens and learn ways to interrupt the cycle. More than anything, I want to feel less shame. I would also like to be able to go out with friends without planning my whole day around food."

**Example 3: Navigating recovery while in school**

"I am a college student, and my eating has been all over the place. I want to stay in school and not let this take over. My goal is to have a routine that supports my studies and my health. I am scared that treatment will take too much time, so I need help figuring out how to fit it in."

**Example 4: Wanting to rebuild relationships**

"My eating disorder has made me isolate from my family. I want to repair those relationships. I also want to be able to eat meals with others without panic. I know that might take a while, but that is what I am working toward."

**Example 5: When you are unsure**

"I do not really know what my goals are. I just know I cannot keep living like this. I am hoping you can help me figure out what to focus on first."

These examples show that goals can be practical, emotional, relational, or exploratory. There is no single right way to describe them.

## What to Expect from the Provider

A qualified provider will listen, ask questions, and may offer their own observations. They might ask about your history, current symptoms, and safety. They may also discuss what types of treatment they offer and whether they are a good fit for your needs. If they are not the right fit, they may refer you to someone else. That is a normal part of finding the right care.

You can ask the provider how they typically work with goals. For example: "How do we decide what to work on first?" or "How will we know if we are making progress?" These questions can help you understand their approach.

It is also okay to ask about practical matters like session length, frequency, cost, and confidentiality. You deserve to have your questions answered.

## Common Challenges and How to Handle Them

- **Feeling embarrassed or ashamed.** Many people feel this way. You can say, "This is hard for me to talk about." A good provider will respond with respect.
- **Not knowing what to say.** You can start with what brought you in today. Even "I am not sure" is a valid starting point.
- **Worrying about being judged.** Treatment should be a safe space. If you feel judged, it is okay to address it or consider a different provider.
- **Feeling ambivalent.** Ambivalence is normal. You can share both sides: "Part of me wants to change, and part of me is scared."
- **Forgetting details.** Write things down beforehand or bring notes. You can also ask to take notes during the meeting.

## Making the Most of the Conversation

- **Be as honest as you can.** You do not have to share everything, but honesty helps the provider help you.
- **Ask for clarification.** If you do not understand something, say so.
- **Take your time.** You can pause, breathe, or ask to slow down.
- **Focus on what matters to you.** Your goals are yours; they do not have to match anyone else's expectations.
- **Consider a follow-up.** You may not cover everything in one meeting. It is okay to continue the conversation.

## Next Steps

After the first meeting, you might feel a range of emotions. Take some time to reflect on how it went. Did you feel heard? Did the provider seem knowledgeable and respectful? Did you get a sense of what treatment might involve? You can use these reflections to decide whether to continue with that provider or seek another opinion.

If you are still exploring options, you may find it helpful to read about [how to prepare for a first consultation](https://eatingdisordertreatmentfinder.com/blog/prepare-for-first-eating-disorder-treatment-consultation) or [how to choose an eating disorder treatment provider](https://eatingdisordertreatmentfinder.com/blog/choosing-an-eating-disorder-treatment-provider). These resources offer additional questions and considerations.

Remember, you are not alone. Many people go through this process, and support is available. With the right provider and a clear sense of your goals, the first meeting can be a meaningful step toward the life you want to build.
