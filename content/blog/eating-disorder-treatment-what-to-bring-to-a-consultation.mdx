# Eating Disorder Treatment: What to Bring to a Consultation

Preparing for a first eating disorder treatment consultation can feel like a lot. You may be managing a mix of emotions, logistical questions, and uncertainty about what the appointment will actually involve. One thing you can control is how prepared you feel walking in. This guide offers a practical checklist for what to bring, along with concrete hypothetical examples clearly labeled as examples, and questions you may want to ask.

This article is general information for people researching eating disorder treatment services. It is not a diagnosis, a treatment plan, or legal advice, and it does not promise any particular outcome. Everyone's situation is different, so please consult qualified professionals about your own care and coverage decisions.

## Why preparation matters

A consultation is often the first extended conversation you have with a provider or intake coordinator. The more clearly you can share your history, current situation, and goals, the more useful that conversation can be. Preparation also helps you ask the questions that matter to you, rather than remembering them after you leave.

You do not need to have everything perfectly organized. A simple folder, a notes app, or a single sheet of paper can be enough. The goal is to reduce friction so you can spend more of the appointment on the conversation itself.

## A practical checklist: what to bring

Below is a checklist you can adapt. Not every item will apply to everyone, and you can add or remove items based on your circumstances.

### 1. Identification and practical documents

- A photo ID, if the provider or facility requests one.
- Your insurance card or plan information, if you plan to use insurance.
- Any referral paperwork or forms the provider asked you to complete in advance.
- A list of current medications, including doses, if you take any.

### 2. Contact and scheduling information

- The provider's name, address, and phone number.
- The name of the person you are scheduled to meet, if you know it.
- A way to take notes, such as a notebook or phone.

### 3. Your health and treatment history

- A brief timeline of when concerns began, if you can identify it.
- Previous treatment experiences, if any, and what was helpful or not helpful.
- Names of other professionals involved in your care, such as a primary care clinician or therapist, if you want to coordinate.

### 4. Your questions and goals

- A short list of what you most want to understand or change.
- Questions about the provider's approach, logistics, and next steps.
- Anything you want the provider to know about your preferences or concerns.

### 5. Support and logistics

- If you want, the name of a support person who could join or be available by phone.
- Notes about transportation, work, school, or caregiving responsibilities that affect scheduling.

## Hypothetical examples (clearly labeled as examples)

These examples are hypothetical and are meant only to illustrate how someone might prepare. They are not real people, real providers, or real outcomes.

**Example A: Preparing a short history.**
*Hypothetical:* A person might write a few bullet points such as: "Concerns started about two years ago. Saw a therapist briefly last year. Currently interested in learning about outpatient options." This kind of brief summary can help start the conversation without requiring a complete narrative.

**Example B: Organizing questions by topic.**
*Hypothetical:* Someone might group questions into categories: "Logistics: How often are appointments? What is the cancellation policy?" "Approach: What does a typical session look like? How do you involve family, if at all?" "Next steps: What would you recommend after this consultation?"

**Example C: Bringing a support person.**
*Hypothetical:* A person might ask a trusted friend to attend the first part of the appointment for note-taking or support, then step out for the remainder. Whether this is possible depends on the provider's policies.

**Example D: Preparing for insurance questions.**
*Hypothetical:* Someone might write down their plan name, member ID, and a few questions such as: "Is this provider in network? What information do you need from me to check benefits?"

## Questions you may want to ask

You do not have to ask everything on this list. Choose the questions that feel most relevant to you.

### About the provider and approach

- What is your experience working with eating concerns?
- How would you describe your approach to treatment?
- How do you decide what level of care might be appropriate to consider?
- How do you involve family members or support people, if at all?

### About logistics and scheduling

- How often would we meet, and for how long?
- What is the typical process after this consultation?
- What is your policy on cancellations or rescheduling?
- Do you offer in-person, remote, or both types of appointments?

### About coordination and communication

- How do you coordinate with other professionals, if I want that?
- How do you share information with me and with others involved in my care?
- What should I do if I have questions between appointments?

### About practical matters

- What information do you need from me to check insurance benefits?
- Are there any forms I should complete before the next appointment?
- What are the next steps if we decide to continue working together?

## How to organize your notes

There is no single right way to prepare. Some people prefer a simple list. Others prefer a folder with sections. A few approaches that may help:

- **One-page summary:** A single sheet with your history, current concerns, and top questions.
- **Question cards:** A few index cards, each with one topic and related questions.
- **Digital notes:** A notes app with headings you can scan quickly.
- **Support person:** Someone who can help you remember details or take notes.

If you feel overwhelmed, it is okay to bring just one or two questions. The consultation is a conversation, not a test.

## What not to worry about

You do not need to have a perfect explanation of what is going on. You do not need to know exactly what kind of treatment you want. You do not need to have all your records in order. A consultation is a starting point. The provider's role is to help you understand options and next steps, not to judge how prepared you are.

It is also okay to say, "I am not sure how to describe this." Many people find that the conversation itself helps clarify what they want to focus on.

## If you are supporting someone else

If you are helping a friend or family member prepare, you can assist without taking over. You might offer to help them write down questions, keep track of paperwork, or arrange transportation. You can also ask them what kind of support they want during the appointment itself, such as sitting in for part of it or waiting nearby.

## A short recap checklist

- ID and insurance information, if relevant.
- Referral or intake forms the provider requested.
- Medication list, if applicable.
- A brief history or timeline, if you want one.
- Your top questions and goals.
- Notes on logistics, such as scheduling constraints.
- Optional: a support person, if that would help.

## When to seek professional guidance

This article is general information and is not a substitute for individualized advice. If you have questions about your own situation, coverage, or care options, please consult qualified professionals, such as a licensed clinician, a registered dietitian, or your insurance plan's member services team. They can help you understand what applies to you.

## A final note

Preparing for a consultation does not require perfection. It requires a little bit of thought about what you want to share and what you want to learn. Bring what you can, ask what you need, and take the conversation one step at a time. If you are unsure where to start, a single question is enough to begin.
