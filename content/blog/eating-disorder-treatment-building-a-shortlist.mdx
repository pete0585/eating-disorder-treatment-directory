## Start With Fit, Not With a Long List

Searching for eating disorder treatment can feel like standing in front of a wall of doors. Every option sounds serious and clinical, and many use similar language. The goal of this guide is not to tell you which door to open. It is to help you build a shortlist of options that fit your situation, so the conversations you have next are more focused and less overwhelming.

This article is informational only. It is not a diagnosis, a treatment recommendation, or legal advice. Eating disorder treatment decisions should be made with qualified professionals who can assess your individual needs.

### What a shortlist is — and what it is not

A shortlist is a small set of programs, clinicians, or care settings you want to learn more about. It is not a ranking, and it is not a substitute for clinical assessment. You can build a shortlist before you have all the answers, then revise it as you learn more.

A useful shortlist usually has three to six options. Fewer than three may leave you without meaningful comparisons. More than six can recreate the same overwhelm you started with.

## Step 1: Define the Practical Boundaries First

Before comparing philosophies or approaches, write down the constraints that are real for you. These are not preferences; they are the conditions any option has to meet to be workable.

- **Location and travel:** How far can you realistically travel, and how often? If daily or several-times-weekly attendance is part of a program, distance matters differently than it does for a weekly appointment.
- **Schedule:** What days and times can you consistently attend? Consistency often matters more than convenience.
- **Support at home:** Who can help with transportation, meals, appointments, or childcare? If no one can, that changes what you need to ask about.
- **Financial and insurance questions:** You do not need to solve coverage before you start, but you do need to know what questions to ask. Ask each option directly about insurance, payment, and what happens if coverage changes.
- **Language, identity, and accessibility needs:** If you need a provider who speaks your language, understands your cultural context, or can accommodate a disability, make that a filter rather than an afterthought.

> Example (hypothetical): A person who works full time and has no paid leave decides that any option requiring four consecutive weeks away from work is not currently workable. That single constraint immediately narrows the list.

## Step 2: Match the Level of Care to the Level of Need — With Professional Guidance

Eating disorder treatment is often described in levels of care, such as outpatient, intensive outpatient, partial hospitalization, residential, and inpatient. These levels differ in intensity, structure, and how much daily life they replace.

You do not need to diagnose yourself to use this framework. Instead, use it to ask better questions:

- What level of care is being recommended for me, and why?
- What would need to change for a different level of care to be appropriate?
- How are transitions between levels handled if my needs change?

A qualified professional — such as a physician, therapist, or dietitian with eating disorder experience — can help you understand which levels of care are appropriate to explore. The purpose of the shortlist is to identify options within the range you and your care team are considering.

> Example (hypothetical): Someone who has been told by a clinician that they need more support than weekly outpatient therapy might shortlist intensive outpatient and partial hospitalization programs, while keeping one outpatient option as a step-down possibility.

## Step 3: Look at What the Program Actually Does

Program descriptions can be broad. To compare them, look for specifics in these areas:

### Assessment and care planning
- How is the initial assessment conducted, and who is involved?
- How often is the care plan reviewed, and who can change it?
- How are family members or support people included, if at all?

### Treatment team and coordination
- Which disciplines are represented on the team (for example, therapy, medical, nutrition, psychiatry)?
- How does the team communicate with each other and with outside providers?
- Who is my main point of contact?

### Day-to-day experience
- What does a typical day or week look like?
- What is the group size, and how are groups structured?
- What meals and snacks are provided or supported, and how is that handled?

### Aftercare and transitions
- What planning happens before discharge?
- How are relapses or setbacks addressed?
- What follow-up support is available after the program ends?

If a program cannot answer these questions clearly, that is useful information. It may mean the details are not standardized, or that you need to speak with a different person.

## Step 4: Ask About Fit, Not Just Availability

Availability matters, but fit matters too. Fit is not about whether a program feels welcoming in a brochure. It is about whether the practical and interpersonal elements line up with your needs.

Consider asking:

- What experience does the team have with people whose situation is similar to mine?
- How do you handle differences in cultural background, gender identity, or religious practice?
- What happens if I do not feel comfortable with a particular group or clinician?
- How do you involve family or support people, and what if I do not want that?

You are not expected to know all the right questions in advance. It is reasonable to say, "I am not sure what to ask — can you walk me through how this usually works?"

## Step 5: Build the Shortlist in Writing

Once you have gathered information, put it in one place. A simple table or list can make patterns easier to see.

### Shortlist checklist

For each option, note:

- [ ] Level of care and typical length of participation
- [ ] Location and format (in person, remote, or hybrid)
- [ ] Schedule and time commitment
- [ ] Treatment team members and how they coordinate
- [ ] How family or support people are involved
- [ ] Insurance, payment, and what happens if coverage changes
- [ ] Assessment process and how the care plan is reviewed
- [ ] Aftercare and follow-up support
- [ ] Questions that remain unanswered

> Example (hypothetical): After filling in this checklist for four programs, someone notices that two of them have unclear aftercare descriptions. That does not automatically remove them, but it becomes a specific question for the next call.

## Step 6: Narrow With a Second Round of Questions

A shortlist is not final. After the first round of information gathering, you will likely have new questions. That is normal.

A second round might focus on:

- The two or three details that matter most to you
- Anything that felt vague or inconsistent
- How the program handles changes in your needs
- What the first week would actually look like

You can also ask whether you can speak with someone who has gone through the program, if the program offers that. Not all do, and that is not necessarily a red flag.

## Common Pitfalls When Building a Shortlist

- **Treating the first option as the only option.** Early information can feel decisive, but it is only one data point.
- **Confusing intensity with quality.** A higher level of care is not automatically better; it is a different level of support.
- **Ignoring logistics until the end.** Transportation, scheduling, and coverage can determine whether a plan is sustainable.
- **Waiting for perfect certainty.** You can make a shortlist, ask questions, and revise it. You do not need to be certain before you start.
- **Trying to do this alone.** A clinician, a trusted person, or a support line can help you keep track of what matters.

## When to Involve a Professional

If you are unsure what level of care is appropriate, if your health may be at risk, or if you feel stuck, involve a qualified professional. This guide is not a substitute for clinical assessment. A physician, therapist, or dietitian with eating disorder experience can help you interpret what you are learning and decide what to do next.

If you are in crisis or need urgent help, contact local emergency services or a crisis line in your area.

## A Shortlist Is a Starting Point

Building a shortlist is a way to turn a large, abstract search into a set of specific, answerable questions. The goal is not to find the perfect option on the first try. It is to find options that fit your real life closely enough to have a meaningful conversation about next steps.

You can start small. Write down your constraints, gather information from a few options, and ask the questions that matter to you. Then revise. The shortlist is yours to change as you learn more.
